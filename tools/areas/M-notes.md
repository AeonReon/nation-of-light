# Writer M notes

## Who owns what among the five beauty ladders
- **beauty.look**: reading ONE building as a whole. Its parts by name, what it is for, bottom/middle/top, roof, door, windows, age, how it suits its weather, and your own judgement with reasons. No symmetry, proportion or structure.
- **beauty.shapes**: exact flat and solid geometry. Constructing circle, hexagon, triangle, right angle, square, 3-4-5; which regular shapes tile (as a fact); nets, Euler's count, the Platonic solids. No pattern design, no buildings.
- **beauty.symmetry**: the three kinds of repeat (mirror, turn, slide) and designing patterns. Mirror lines, turning order, snowflake, borders, patterns at home and around the world, a sliding tile you design. The one building rung is about symmetry only.
- **beauty.proportion**: ratios and scale. Halves and thirds by eye, rectangles as numbers, A-paper, body-sized things, the door as a measure, windows per floor, the golden ratio tested honestly, book margins, scale drawings, designing to a chosen ratio.
- **beauty.arches**: structure. Beam against arch, corbel against true arch, keystone, outward push, the hanging chain, arch shapes, domes, columns and their parts, column traditions, why arches let columns stand further apart.
- Kept clear of beauty.town (streets, materials, light, trees, the walk), classic.beautiful, make.draw (pencil measuring, perspective), belong.rooms (going into grand rooms), home.mend and make.wood.

## beauty.look: Looking at buildings (14 rungs, everybody, side by side)
Order: name five parts of the building you are in (doable anywhere) → what a building is for → bottom/middle/top → roofs → the main door → windows → guess the age and check → homes built for other weather (photos) → describe one to somebody who cannot see it → three liked and three not → what each three share → who built one → five rules of your own → five minutes talking somebody through a building. The vocabulary builds rung by rung and the last two rungs use all of it.
Unsure: roof-and-weather line kept soft ("common ... many exceptions"). Shirakawa-go snow roofs and courtyard houses I am confident of.

## beauty.shapes: Shapes and geometry (12 rungs, from about nine; no `from`)
Order: find shapes → string circle → centre by folding → hexagon from six radii → triangle from alternate corners → folded right angle → square from a rectangle → 3-4-5 string → why only triangles, squares and hexagons tile (angle sums) → cube net → Euler's F + V - E = 2 → icosahedron net folded. Each construction uses the one before (string circle → hexagon → triangle → triangle template for the icosahedron; folded right angle checks the square and the 3-4-5 corner).
Facts checked: radius steps round six times (chord = radius at 60°); fold-through-centre; double fold gives 90°; 9+16=25 and the right angle sits between the 3 and the 4 (the third mark); corners 60/90/120 fill 360, pentagon 108 cannot; eleven cube nets; cross net valid; Euler cube 6+8-12, square pyramid 5+5-8, icosahedron 20+12-30; Euler 1750s; five Platonic solids; strip-of-ten-plus-five-and-five icosahedron net.
Unsure: none on the maths. "Plato wrote about all five" (Timaeus) is right.

## beauty.symmetry: Symmetry and pattern (14 rungs, from about six; no `from`)
Order: fold-and-cut → mirror lines in the room → check with a mirror (and the face that is not symmetrical) → almost symmetrical → complete a half on a grid → turning symmetry and counting its order → six-armed snowflake → sliding border → name the repeat in a pattern at home → a building front and what breaks its mirror → patterns from six cultures → a slide tile (Escher method) → tile it twelve times → colour and put it up.
Fixed a common error: a paper snowflake needs half, then thirds, then half again (12 layers); half and thirds alone gives only three-fold turning. The note says so.
Unsure: the claim is only that Alhambra tiles mix repeat types (I avoided the disputed "all 17 wallpaper groups"). Removed "why they calm a room" from the old line: a claim, not a fact.

## beauty.proportion: Proportion (18 rungs, `from: 10`)
Order: halves by eye → thirds → rectangles as a number → same shape at two sizes (diagonal check) → A-paper halves → body-sized steps, seats, tables → door as a two-metre measure → windows per floor → Fibonacci ratios to 1.618 → four rectangles shown to two people → test one famous golden-ratio claim → book margins → lay out a page → scale drawing of a table → scale drawing of a real front → change one proportion and ask → design a front to one ratio → neat scale drawing explained.
Golden ratio: said only that the ratios approach 1.618, that preference tests over ~150 years are mixed, that most famous claims (Parthenon, pyramid, Mona Lisa, faces) depend on which edges you choose with little evidence of intent, and that Le Corbusier's Modulor did use it on purpose.
Facts checked: Fibonacci quotients listed; 1202 Liber Abaci, known earlier in India; A4 210×297, ratio √2 ≈ 1.41, half upright on upright whole shares a diagonal; Letter 8.5×11 half is a different shape; inch rectangles 4 / 5.7 / 6.5 / 8.
Less sure, rough on purpose: step heights "most between about 15 and 20 cm"; chair seat near the back of the knee; table near seated elbows; doors "close to two metres"; about three metres floor to floor; older European town houses with the tallest windows one floor up; bottom margin widest in many well-made books.

## beauty.arches: Arches and columns (14 rungs, everybody, a grown-up cuts thick card)
Order: paper flat against paper curved → beams at home → a real arch and where its weight goes → corbel arch from books (no support needed, so it comes first) → true arch of seven card wedges on a support → keystone and any block → outward push at the feet → hanging chain → arch shapes worldwide → eggshell domes → parts of a column → columns from five traditions → beam gaps against arch gaps → an arch with no glue carrying a book, explained.
Removed the old "a round arch and a pointed one, say which is older": wrong worldwide, since pointed arches were used in the Islamic world before Gothic Europe.
Facts checked: Maya corbel vaults; Newgrange chamber corbelled and older than Giza; Hooke 1670s; Gaudí hanging models; Pont du Gard; Great Mosque of Córdoba horseshoe arches; Zhaozhou Bridge over 1,400 years; Karnak papyrus columns; Persepolis double-bull capitals; Chinese bracket sets; Greek Doric with no base; any block removed collapses a dry arch.
Unsure: whether seven card wedges of glued box card stand reliably; the note offers glued layers and salt dough. The paper-arch coin count varies a lot with how well the ends are braced.

## home.tools: Tools (16 rungs, `from: 8`)
Order: find five → write five safety rules → safety glasses and gloves (before anything is hit) → tape measure → spirit level → pliers → spanner → hammer a nail (peg holds it) → pull a nail → craft knife (grown-ups or a teenager with one; a child uses scissors) → tell drill bits apart (a child can, by looking) → drill one hole (grown-ups only, child watches in glasses) → clean, oil, store → one real job → four weeks of putting back → teach somebody to carry, pass and store.
Kept out: sawing, drilling where you meant, screws flush (make.wood); screwdriver fit, never-yourself repairs (home.mend). No twists existed on these rungs, so none kept.
Unsure: whether any hand-tool rung should be `with` rather than `kit` for a child. I kept `kit` because a grown-up can do them alone, and every note names where a child needs a grown-up. "No gloves with a drill" and "wires run above, below and beside switches and sockets" are standard safety advice, but wiring layouts differ by country; the note says "often" and "if in doubt, ask somebody qualified".
