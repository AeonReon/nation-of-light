# Writer Y notes: the under-sevens, make.scissors and make.crayons

Both to BRIEF-LITTLE.md and the `food.kitchen` model: 12 rungs, every rung `ctx: "with"`,
`mins` on rung 1 only, a `note` to the grown-up on every rung, `how` only where the doing needs
steps, `who` and `about` added, `line` rewritten to end at the last rung.

Shared decision: I dropped the old "From four, beside you." prefix from both `line` fields, because
the new `who` label ("From about four, with a grown-up beside them", 44 chars) now says it and the
two lines read as a repetition side by side on the row. If the owner wants the prefix kept for the
little strand, it goes back at the front of `line` with no other change.

## make.scissors — Scissors (12 rungs, safety is rungs 1–4)

Order: open and shut in the air · the grip · carrying and handing over · a fringe of snips · a piece
of paper cut in two · a drawn straight line · a square, turning the paper · a curvy line · a circle ·
fold, cut, open it out · three shapes stuck down as a picture · tell somebody what it is.

- **Two changes to the old order.** (1) The fringe moved up, above "cut a piece of paper in two".
  A fringe is one open-and-shut per snip with no forward movement at all, so it is the easiest
  cutting there is; cutting right through a sheet needs the paper fed forward by the other hand and
  the blades not shut completely. The old order had the harder job first. (2) The old rung 9, "turn
  the paper instead of the scissors", is not a rung of its own any more — it is the technique that
  makes a square possible, so it is inside rung 7 (the square) as the test, the `how` and the note,
  and it carries through the curve and the circle after it. That freed the slot the fringe move
  needed, and the count stays at twelve.
- **Safety, all in the first third.** Rung 1's note is which scissors: child-sized (about as long as
  a grown-up's hand), rounded ends, metal blades that actually cut — all-plastic ones chew paper and
  make the job harder rather than safer. It also carries the one rule said out loud once (paper and
  nothing else: not hair, not clothes, not anything with a wire in it) and sitting at a table.
  Rung 3 is carrying and passing as its own rung: blades shut in the fist, points at the floor,
  walking, handles turned towards the other person before letting go; between cuts the scissors go
  down shut, and they are put away out of reach at the end, which matters most with a smaller child
  in the house. I made rung 3 `kind: "kindness"` — handles first is a rung about the other person.
- **Left-handed.** In rung 1's note, as part of choosing the pair: a child who reaches with the left
  hand needs left-handed scissors, because in a right-handed pair the upper blade sits over the cut
  line and they cannot see where the cut is going. Rung 6 (the first drawn line) says again that this
  is where it starts to matter — rungs 1–5 have no line to see, so a family without a left-handed
  pair can still climb the first third honestly while they find one.
- **"Thumb on top" is right** and I kept the test. Held properly the thumb is in the small upper hole
  pointing up, two fingers in the lower hole, and the helper hand holds the paper thumb-up as well.
  The note is written so a grown-up can correct the grip **without stopping the child**: draw a mark
  on the top handle and say the mark goes up, or turn their hand while they carry on cutting. Taking
  the scissors off a child to fix the hold ends the work, and it is never needed. Note also says both
  the middle and index finger in the lower hole is fine for small hands.
- **Nothing bought.** Paper for every rung is an envelope, a magazine page, card off a food packet or
  a letter that came through the door; stiff paper is named as the easy one for the fringe and thin
  paper for the folded rung. Lines and shapes are drawn by the grown-up with a thick dark pen — a
  wide line means anywhere inside it is right, which is the single most useful free trick on the
  ladder. Rung 9's circle is drawn round a cup or a jar lid. Rung 11 needs glue: flour and water
  stirred to a thick cream, or a few pieces of tape.
- **Twists** (there were none left on this ladder in school.json, though v45 says it once had the hand
  twist): "The hand you would never choose." on the fringe (rung 4) and the curve (rung 8), and
  "Fold it twice instead of once." on the folded rung (rung 10). I did not put the other-hand twist on rungs 6,
  7 or 9 — four twists on one ladder is enough, and I kept them off the rungs where a grown-up
  climbing beside the child is most likely to be holding a shape steady.
- **Unsure about:** rung 10 (fold and cut) is the one rung where a four-year-old may not have the
  squeeze for two layers; the note says use thin paper, but it may sit better swapped with rung 11.
  I would also accept the argument that carrying and passing (rung 3) belongs at rung 1 — I put the
  open-and-shut first only because it is the one-minute rung that gets the tool into their hand.

## make.crayons — Crayons (12 rungs; drawing now comes before colouring in)

Order: fill a whole page · a long line and a short one · a circle that joins up · a square · a
triangle · colour inside a shape **you drew yourself** · fill it with no white left · press hard and
softly · a whole picture in more than one colour · a person with a face · something that happened
today · give the picture to somebody and say what is in it.

- **The order question, answered: shapes come first.** The old ladder had four rungs of colouring in
  (1–4) and then the circle, square and triangle at 5–6. In the room it happens the other way round.
  A child covers paper and draws closed shapes long before they can hold a crayon inside somebody
  else's outline, and "no white bits left" is a finishing job that needs more control than a circle
  does, not less. So drawing is rungs 2–5 and colouring in starts at rung 6 — and rung 6 is
  deliberately **their own** shape, so the first colouring-in is inside a line they made, with a line
  somebody else drew arriving only at rung 9. Square before triangle: two slanted lines and three
  corners is the harder one.
- **Rung 1 is the grip rung.** Filling a page is the job; the note is: break the crayons in half
  first. A short crayon cannot be held in a fist, so the fingers find the hold on their own and no
  correcting is needed. Broken crayons cost nothing and this is the only equipment advice on the
  ladder.
- **Nothing bought — what stands in for a colouring book.** Said plainly in rung 6 and again in rung
  9: the grown-up outlines something on plain paper with a thick dark pen (a house, a fish, a tree, a
  face, big and simple), or they draw round real objects — a cup, a lid, a spoon, a hand — to make
  shapes worth filling in. Printed shapes are stood in for the same way: four dots to join for a
  square, three for a triangle. Paper is the back of a letter, a paper bag or a food packet opened
  out; the marker can be a crayon, a coloured pencil, a pen, or chalk on a paving stone outside.
- **Two merges to make room.** The old 7 and 8 (a person, then a face and who it is) are one rung
  now, and the old 9 and 10 (your house, then somebody outside it) are gone as separate rungs — a
  house is named as an example in the whole-picture note, and the scene work is carried by rung 11,
  "something that happened today", which is a better rung because the child chooses the subject.
- **The ending is different from Scissors on purpose.** Scissors ends with telling somebody about the
  picture (courage). Crayons ends with **giving** it away and saying what is in it (kindness), so the
  two ladders in the same category do not finish with the same rung. `line` matches it.
- **Twists:** "The hand you would never choose." on the line, the circle and the person; "The side of
  the crayon, not the point." on the hard-and-soft rung, which is a real technique and not only a
  twist.
- **Unsure about:** whether rung 2 (lines) earns a rung at all for a child who already scribbles — I
  kept it because it is the first rung where the child decides where a mark stops, and because it
  gives a reachable step between covering a page and closing a circle. Rung 7 ("no white bits") is
  the rung most likely to be given up on; the note says start with a small shape and hold it up to
  the window to find the gaps. Rung 10's "a head with legs coming straight out of it is a common
  first version" is written as what happens at the table, not as a developmental claim.
